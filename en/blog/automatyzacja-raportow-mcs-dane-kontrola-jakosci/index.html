Swimming MCS Reports: Why One Spreadsheet Takes So Many Evenings

A club ranking position is not the same as a swimmer's actual finishing place at a meet.

Why the report took so long

In Poznań, preparing an MCS report for around 120 swimmers involved up to ten coaches and many evenings of repetitive spreadsheet work.

What the report requires

The format I worked with required relevant meets, swimmers, events and actual finishing places, not swimming times. Meets were chronological and athletes alphabetical.

The Swimrankings export trap

A position in a club-specific Swimrankings export may be a ranking within that club rather than the athlete's finishing place in the overall meet. Copying it into the MCS report without verification would be incorrect.

When exports fail

Large Excel exports can fail. Smaller date ranges may be combined, but they need deduplication and ordering checks, and still do not solve the placement problem.

Structuring thousands of entries

Each athlete needs enough rows for their maximum number of starts at one meet. With many athletes and competitions, preparing the structure is substantial work.

Ten coaches, one file

Different editing and formatting habits make merged spreadsheets inconsistent. A repeatable structure reduces correction and review time.

Verify actual placements

Actual results must be checked for each event and meet. An anonymised comparison illustrates why club export positions cannot substitute for official finishing places.

Why automate?

Excel formulas and Python reduced repetitive work. Further automation helped limit manual transcription, allowing coaches to spend less time on administrative tasks.

Conclusion

Reliable MCS reports depend on correct competition placements and consistent structure. Automation can reduce effort, but it must preserve the distinction between club rankings and meet results.
