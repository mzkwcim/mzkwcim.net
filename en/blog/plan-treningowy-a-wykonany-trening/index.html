Training Plan vs. Completed Workout: Why They Are Not the Same

A training plan describes intent, not necessarily what happened in the pool.

The plan is not the record

A coach plans distances, repetitions, intensity, rest and the purpose of each set. Lane availability, fatigue or technical corrections may require adjustments. A changed plan is not necessarily a failure; treating the original plan as a record of completed work is the problem.

Twelve times 25 metres

A set of 12 × 25 metres totals 300 metres. If a swimmer completes eight repetitions, that is 200 metres, or roughly 67% of planned volume. Yet different pacing or rest intervals can change the stimulus even when the distance is identical.

What should be recorded?

For important sets, record planned and completed repetitions, distance, intensity, rest and reasons for modifications. Context helps distinguish deliberate coaching decisions from incomplete work.

Why 90% can be misleading

Removing 300 metres of easy swimming from a 3,000-metre session is not equivalent to removing 300 metres of high-intensity work. Both leave 90% of planned distance, but not necessarily 90% of the intended stimulus.

Patterns matter

One adjustment may be entirely reasonable. Repeatedly shortening the same type of set raises useful questions about time, organisation, recovery and workload. The data points to a question; it does not prove a cause.

Swimtopia and training execution

Swimtopia compares planned and completed volume across weeks. Such a chart highlights quantitative differences but does not automatically assess training quality or physiological adaptation.

Attendance is another measure

Presence at the pool, completed work and the resulting adaptation are three different questions. See the related article about attendance and missed training stimuli.

Conclusion

The plan supports decisions before the session. The execution record supports decisions afterwards. Understanding the difference helps coaches avoid conclusions based on one percentage.
